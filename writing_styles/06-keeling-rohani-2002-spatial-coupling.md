# 06 — Keeling & Rohani (2002), *Ecology Letters*
**"Estimating spatial coupling in epidemiological systems: a mechanistic approach"**
*Ecol Lett* 5(1):20–29. doi:10.1046/j.1461-0248.2002.00268.x.

> **Basis of this analysis.** The full text is paywalled with no open-access copy (checked via PMC, Europe PMC, Unpaywall, OpenAlex and Semantic Scholar, September 2026); the article is not indexed in Europe PMC at all. This file is built on the **verbatim abstract** and the bibliographic record.

**Archetype:** the *justify-the-shortcut* methods paper — take a phenomenological term that everyone already uses, derive it from an explicit mechanistic process, and say under what conditions the shortcut is valid. A two-author, ten-page contribution whose value is licence to keep using a simple model.

## The abstract, verbatim
> "In recent years, ecologists and epidemiologists have paid increasing attention to the influence of spatial structure in shaping the dynamics and determining the persistence of populations. This is fundamentally affected by the concept of 'coupling'—the flux of individuals moving between separate populations. In this paper, we contrast how coupling is typically implemented in epidemic models with more detailed approaches. Our aim is to link the popular phenomenological formulations with the results of mechanistic models. By concentrating on the behaviour of simple epidemic systems, we relate explicit movement patterns with observed levels of coupling, validating the standard formulation. The analysis is then extended to include a brief study of how the correlation between stochastic populations is affected by coupling, the underlying deterministic dynamics and the relative population sizes."

## What the abstract teaches
The six sentences form a **methods-paper skeleton** that is unusually clean:

1. **Establish the live interest.** "In recent years, ecologists and epidemiologists have paid increasing attention to the influence of spatial structure…"
2. **Name and define the object in one clause, with the definition set off by a dash.** "the concept of 'coupling'—the flux of individuals moving between separate populations." Quotation marks on first use, definition immediately, never again.
3. **State the contrast that motivates the work.** "we contrast how coupling is typically implemented in epidemic models with more detailed approaches." The gap is between *what people do* and *what is defensible*, which is the most common real gap in methods work.
4. **State the aim as a verb of connection, not of replacement.** "Our aim is to link the popular phenomenological formulations with the results of mechanistic models." The paper is not proposing to overthrow the standard formulation.
5. **State the strategy and the verdict together.** "By concentrating on the behaviour of simple epidemic systems, we relate explicit movement patterns with observed levels of coupling, validating the standard formulation." **The result is that the shortcut is fine** — a validating result, published as such. This is worth noting: confirming existing practice is a publishable contribution when the confirmation is derived rather than assumed.
6. **Flag the secondary analysis honestly as secondary.** "The analysis is then extended to include a brief study of how the correlation between stochastic populations is affected by coupling, the underlying deterministic dynamics and the relative population sizes." The word "brief" is doing real work: it tells the reader not to expect a full treatment.

## Framing conventions to take from this paper
- **"Our aim is to link X with Y."** Use this when your contribution is a bridge between an approximation people use and the process it approximates. It scopes the paper precisely and promises no more than it delivers.
- **Deliberate simplification as method**: "By concentrating on the behaviour of simple epidemic systems…" — the tractable case is chosen so the relationship can be derived, and that choice is announced in the abstract.
- **Validate rather than debunk.** Papers that show a common approximation is safe are cited for decades. Say "validating the standard formulation" plainly rather than burying it.
- **Signal the weight of each analysis.** "a brief study of" is an honest register marker; use it instead of letting a secondary analysis masquerade as a main result.
