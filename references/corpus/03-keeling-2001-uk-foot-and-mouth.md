## The abstract, verbatim
> "Foot-and-mouth is one of the world's most economically important livestock diseases. We developed an individual farm-based stochastic model of the current UK epidemic. The fine grain of the epidemiological data reveals the infection dynamics at an unusually high spatiotemporal resolution. We show that the spatial distribution, size, and species composition of farms all influence the observed pattern and regional variability of outbreaks. The other key dynamical component is long-tailed stochastic dispersal of infection, combining frequent local movements with occasional long jumps. We assess the history and possible duration of the epidemic, the performance of control strategies, and general implications for disease dynamics in space and time."

## What the abstract teaches
Seven sentences, each doing one job — this is a template worth borrowing as an outline (the structure, not the sentences):

1. **Why anyone should care, in one clause.** "one of the world's most economically important livestock diseases." Importance is asserted in the register the audience uses (economic, not epidemiological).
2. **What was built, with its two defining adjectives.** "an individual farm-based stochastic model" — unit of analysis and stochasticity, both named. Note "of the current UK epidemic": the paper is explicitly about a live event.
3. **Why the data make the analysis possible.** "The fine grain of the epidemiological data reveals the infection dynamics at an unusually high spatiotemporal resolution." The data resolution is presented as the enabling condition, and "unusually" claims novelty for the dataset rather than for the method.
4. **The first finding, as a list of drivers.** "the spatial distribution, size, and species composition of farms all influence the observed pattern and regional variability of outbreaks."
5. **The second finding, named as a mechanism.** "long-tailed stochastic dispersal of infection, combining frequent local movements with occasional long jumps." The mechanism is given a memorable two-part description; "long-tailed" is the technical claim, "frequent local movements with occasional long jumps" is its plain-English gloss in the same sentence.
6. **What is delivered, in three registers.** "the history and possible duration of the epidemic, the performance of control strategies, and general implications for disease dynamics in space and time" — retrospective, prospective/operational, and general-theoretical. A modelling paper that offers all three is hard to dismiss as either purely academic or purely operational.

Two verbs are doing careful work: **"assess"** (not "predict") for duration and control performance, and **"possible duration"** — a live-epidemic forecast is framed as a range of possibilities from the first mention.

## Distinctive moves to borrow
- **Heterogeneity is the finding.** The title puts "heterogeneous landscape" in it; the argument is that spatial structure and farm composition, not just a transmission rate, determine the epidemic. Where your system is heterogeneous, make the heterogeneity a result rather than a nuisance parameter.
- **Dispersal kernels with fat tails** are described in words as well as in parameters. "Frequent local movements with occasional long jumps" is the sentence a policymaker remembers; the power-law exponent is not.
- **Working on a live epidemic is stated, not hidden.** "the current UK epidemic" dates the work and sets expectations about data completeness.
