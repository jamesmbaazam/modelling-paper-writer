# 46 — Griffin et al. (2015), *Proceedings of the Royal Society B*
**"Gradual acquisition of immunity to severe malaria with increasing exposure"**
Griffin JT, Hollingsworth TD, Reyburn H, Drakeley CJ, Riley EM, Ghani AC. *Proc R Soc B* 282(1801):20142657. doi:10.1098/rspb.2014.2657.

**Archetype:** *competing hypotheses adjudicated by a mechanistic fit* — two readings of the same literature (immunity acquired after one or two infections, versus gradually) are expressed as a model, fitted to age- and transmission-stratified data, and separated. The corpus's malaria exemplar and its example of a *Proc B* biology-journal register.

## Structure
*Proc R Soc B*: `Abstract` → `Keywords` → **`1. Background`** (not "Introduction") → `2. Material and methods` with **lettered subsections** — *(a) Tanzanian study*, *(b) …* → `3. Results` → `4. Discussion` → `Data accessibility` → `Funding statement` → `Conflict of interests`.

**Royal Society papers number sections and letter their subsections**, and use `Background` where other journals use `Introduction`. The back matter is short, mandatory and separately headed — data accessibility, funding with grant numbers per author initials, conflicts — which is a cleaner pattern than burying these in acknowledgements.

## Opening move
The abstract opens on the contradiction the paper exists to resolve, naming both sides and the evidence behind each:
> "Previous analyses have suggested that immunity to non-cerebral severe malaria due to *Plasmodium falciparum* is acquired after only a few infections, whereas longitudinal studies show that some children experience multiple episodes of severe disease, suggesting that immunity may not be acquired so quickly."

**Two bodies of evidence that cannot both be right, stated in one sentence.** This is the expectation-violation opening in its adjudication form: not *theory predicts X and data show Y*, but *these data and those data disagree, and a model can say which reading survives*.

`1. Background` then builds the disagreement carefully, flagging what is settled and what is not — "Immunity to severe malaria is known to develop with repeat infections of *P. falciparum*, but the rate of acquisition of immunity is a subject of on-going study" — before naming the confounder that makes the question hard:
> "Age patterns of disease are further confounded by variation in exposure to *P. falciparum* and immune responses, resulting in a high degree in variability in the number of disease episodes among children living in the same area."

**Name the confounder between the two candidate explanations in the introduction.** Age and exposure are collinear in any single setting, which is precisely why the paper needs data across transmission intensities.

## Methods
- **Summarise a previously published dataset and point at the full description**: "Full details of the Tanzanian study are published elsewhere but are summarized briefly here." Then give what a reader needs to judge this analysis — collection period, number of hospitals, totals and outcomes: "data from 1989 people with documented evidence of severe malaria… and known place of residence were included in this study, of whom 134 (6.7%) had a fatal outcome."
- **Explain why this population is the right natural experiment**, in one sentence:
  > "The population in this area is culturally and ethnically homogeneous, but exposed to highly variable levels of *P. falciparum* transmission intensity, which depend on the altitude from sea level to above 1800 m."

  **Homogeneous in the confounders, variable in the exposure** — the design argument for the whole paper, made in 30 words.
- **Give the clinical case definitions in full, with thresholds, instruments and exclusions.** Each outcome is operationalised so another group could reproduce the classification: severe malarial anaemia as parasitaemia "with haemoglobin concentration (Hb) less than 5 g dl⁻¹ (HaemoCue AB, Ängelholm, Sweden)"; cerebral malaria as parasitaemia "with Blantyre coma score (BCS) less than 4 and, if there was reduced response to pain, blood glucose level greater than 38 mg dl⁻¹ (2.1 mmol l⁻¹), no convulsions within 1 h of diagnosis and no anticonvulsants administered within 6 h of diagnosis."
- **Name the residual category rather than dropping it**: "Patients who did not meet any of these criteria but who met the criteria for inclusion in the study reported in are classified as 'other severe malaria' cases."
- **State the stratification that will carry the inference**: wards were classified "into one of six groups of differing transmission intensity: below 600 m, 600–1200 m and more than 1200 m.a.s.l." in each of two districts.

## Results
**Describe the exposure gradient and the data before the model**, so the reader can see what the model must reproduce: "People in the Tanzanian study lived at a wide range of altitudes, leading to a high degree of variability in malaria risk, with hospitals placed in areas of highest population density."

**Report the composition of the outcome with overlaps quantified** — important when syndromes co-occur:
> "Just under half (47%) of the severe malaria cases, and 78% of cases who died, presented with at least one of cerebral malaria, severe malarial anaemia or respiratory distress, and 8% of cases were admitted with more than one of these syndromes. The combination of respiratory distress and anaemia accounted for 60% of all cases with more than one syndrome."

**Give the profile of one stratum in full** so the contrast across strata is concrete: "In the highest transmission area (Tanga, altitude below 600 m), the majority of admissions were in the youngest age groups (43% aged below 1 year), the most common manifestation was severe malarial anaemia (47% of cases) and 3% of cases presented with cerebral malaria."

**Claim the fit across settings, not within one**: "The best-fitting mathematical model captured the differences in admission patterns by age across the wide range of settings." For a model fitted simultaneously across strata, the fit *across* strata is the evidence; a within-stratum fit would be unremarkable.

## The adjudication, and how it is reported
The headline is a correction to the received estimate, stated with the methodological reason it differs:
> "In accordance with previous studies, our analysis shows an increase in immunity with cumulative exposure. However, by including multiple syndromes and fitting a model across multiple transmission intensities simultaneously we estimate a more gradual acquisition of immunity to severe disease than previously suggested."

**Agree with prior work on the direction, then differ on the magnitude, and name the two design features that explain the difference.** This is how to correct a field without attacking it.

The second finding separates two mechanisms that produce the same observable pattern:
> "While the overall risk of severe disease varies with force of infection, we were able to explain patterns of severe disease syndromes using a model of a solely physiological, age-dependent process."

Incidence is exposure-driven; which syndrome presents is age-driven. **Partitioning one observed pattern between two mechanisms, and saying which drives which component, is the strongest result a mechanistic fit can deliver.**

**Then the mechanism is walked through at two transmission intensities**, so the reader can follow the logic without the equations:
> "In a high-transmission setting, the first 30 infections are experienced very rapidly, and each individual infection has a low probability of progressing to severe disease episodes as initially infants are protected by maternal immunity, and then immunity is acquired rapidly. In a lower-transmission setting (e.g. EIR = 2), infections are experienced more slowly, and therefore maternal immunity is less important, but age-related gains in immune system competence decrease the proportion of infections that result in severe malaria."

## Literature
Author–year citations, numbered reference list, dense in Background and Discussion. Prior work is cited for the specific claim being tested ("Previous analysis suggested that immunity to non-cerebral severe malaria is acquired after only one or two infections") and the contrary evidence cited immediately after. The Discussion situates the finding against named reviews rather than summarising them.

## Voice
First-person plural, past for the analysis, present for the implications. Inference verbs are carefully graded between what the model shows and what it merely permits: "our analysis shows an increase in immunity"; "The model also **suggests** that physiological changes, rather than exposure, **may** alter the symptoms of disease"; "we were able to explain patterns… using a model of". The abstract's key secondary claim is stated as a conditional prediction — "suggesting that a later age at infection would be associated with a higher proportion of cases presenting with cerebral malaria regardless of exposure" — which is falsifiable and does not overreach the fit.

## Discussion and limitations
Opens by stating the mechanism the analysis supports, in one sentence that contains the whole result:
> "Our analysis demonstrates that variations in patterns of severe malaria with age and transmission intensity can be explained by exposure- and age-driven acquisition of immunity, which determine the incidence of severe malaria, coupled with age-dependent physiological changes, which determine the dominant clinical syndrome of severe malaria."

The limitations are a model of how to interrogate a data source rather than disclaim it — all four concern hospital admission data, and each names a direction:
- **Access and care-seeking**: "whether people with severe malaria come to the hospital at all, and how severely ill they are when they arrive, will be affected by access, both spatial and economic, and treatment-seeking behaviour. Hospital data may thus not be representative of patterns of disease in the community, and in particular, **may under-represent disease incidence and presentation in infants and very young children** who are less able to attract attention to their illness."
- **Differential survival to admission, by syndrome**: "severe anaemia has a slower and less dramatic presentation than cerebral malaria, which may be more likely to cause death before effective treatment can be obtained, and this may result in biased estimates of relationships with age and exposure."
- **An instrument that fails in the youngest children** — a measurement limitation specific to the age gradient being estimated: "the BCS, used to define cerebral malaria, may underestimate the incidence of this syndrome in infants, particularly below eight months of age, as they have not yet fully developed the ability to localize pain."
- **A missing variable**: "previous or subsequent episodes of mild or severe disease were not recorded for these cases."

**Then the ideal study is named, and the reason it would not settle the question is given** — a rare and valuable move:
> "Ideally we would test our model predictions in longitudinal studies incorporating severe and uncomplicated malaria and asymptomatic exposure into the model framework. However, the resulting close monitoring and active case detection of malaria are likely to result in prompt and e[ffective treatment]…"

Closes on the operational consequence, which is what makes the gradual-immunity finding matter: "Careful monitoring of the decline in immunity associated with reduced transmission will therefore be needed to ensure rebound epidemics of severe and fatal malaria are avoided." **If immunity is acquired gradually, falling transmission creates a susceptible older population** — the policy implication follows directly from the parameter the paper revised.

## Data, code and funding
Short, separately headed back matter: data in the supplementary information, funding with per-author grant numbers, declared absence of conflicts, and ethics naming both approving committees and the consent route — "Ethical approval for the study was granted by the ethical committees of the National Institute for Medical Research, Dar es Salaam, Tanzania, and the London School of Hygiene and Tropical Medicine, London, UK."

## Distinctive moves to borrow
1. **Open on two bodies of evidence that disagree**, and position the model as the thing that adjudicates.
2. **Name the confounder** that makes the question hard before describing the design that breaks it.
3. **Argue the natural experiment in one sentence**: homogeneous in the confounders, variable in the exposure.
4. **Give clinical case definitions with thresholds, instruments and exclusions**, and name the residual category.
5. **Claim the fit across strata**, not within, when the model is fitted simultaneously across them.
6. **Agree with prior work on direction, differ on magnitude, and name the design features that explain the difference.**
7. **Partition one observed pattern between two mechanisms** — incidence exposure-driven, syndrome age-driven.
8. **Walk the mechanism through at two parameter values** so the argument survives without the equations.
9. **Report the overlap structure of composite outcomes** rather than treating syndromes as exclusive.
10. **Interrogate the data source four ways, each with a direction of bias**, including an instrument that fails in exactly the subgroup under study.
11. **Name the ideal study and why it still would not settle the question.**
12. **Close on the operational consequence that follows from the revised parameter** — gradual immunity implies rebound risk as transmission falls.
