# 05 — Altizer, Dobson, Hosseini, Hudson, Pascual & Rohani (2006), *Ecology Letters*
**"Seasonality and the dynamics of infectious diseases"**
*Ecology Letters* 9(4):467–484. doi:10.1111/j.1461-0248.2005.00879.x.

**Archetype:** the *mechanism-inventory review* — take one driver (seasonality) and enumerate the biologically distinct mechanisms through which it acts, then draw out the consequences for dynamics and for control. The natural companion to [14-baker-2021-infectious-disease-global-change](14-baker-2021-infectious-disease-global-change.md) and [15-heesterbeek-2015-models-global-health](15-heesterbeek-2015-models-global-health.md).

## Structure
*Ecology Letters* `REVIEWS AND SYNTHESES`: `Abstract` (unstructured, ~250 words) → `Keywords` → `INTRODUCTION` → **`MULTIPLE SEASONAL PROCESSES INFLUENCE HOST–PARASITE SYSTEMS`** → `SEASONAL VARIATION AND SPATIAL PATTERNS OF DISEASE` → `IMPLICATIONS FOR DISEASE EMERGENCE AND CONTROL` → **`FUTURE CHALLENGES FOR MODELLING AND EMPIRICAL STUDIES`** (with imperative sub-headings) → `ACKNOWLEDGEMENTS` → **`GLOSSARY`** → `REFERENCES`.

Three structural decisions make this a model review:

- **The section headings are claims, not topics.** *Multiple seasonal processes influence host–parasite systems* is the paper's central assertion, used as a heading.
- **A `GLOSSARY` carries the vocabulary** so the main text never stops to define: *Bifurcation diagram*, *ENSO*, *External forcing*, *Herd immunity*, *Hypobiosis*, *Multi-annual cycles*. Each entry is one or two sentences in plain language — "External forcing: a factor affecting a dynamical system which is not internal, with no feedback to system variables of interest." **When a review spans two fields that do not share terminology, a glossary is worth more than any amount of in-text explanation.**
- **The research agenda is the final section, with imperative sub-headings** — *Identify how seasonality affects processes mechanistically* · *Describe patterns of seasonality in time-series data* · *Develop manipulative field and laboratory approaches* · *Be aware of evolution*. A reader can take the agenda alone and have a research programme.

**A large comparative table does the enumerating.** Table 1 lists disease after disease with its seasonal pattern in the host's own terms — "Increases during fall and winter", "During dry conditions following", "Peak transmission during" — so the review's claim of generality is demonstrated rather than asserted.

## Opening move
> "Seasonal changes are cyclic, largely predictable, and arguably represent the strongest and most ubiquitous source of external variation influencing human and natural systems."

Note "arguably": the strongest claim in the paper's first sentence is explicitly marked as contestable. The paragraph then states the obstacle, and only then why this system is the place to study it:

> "Despite the pervasive nature of seasonality, exploring its consequences for population dynamics poses a challenge for ecologists, in part because seasonal mechanisms can be difficult to pinpoint empirically and can generate complex population fluctuations. One area where the role of seasonality has been relatively well explored is in the population dynamics of infectious diseases."

Pattern: **ubiquitous phenomenon → why it is hard → the one system where it has been done well → therefore general lessons.** The last step is stated as the paper's purpose in the abstract: "by focusing on well-studied examples of infectious diseases, we hope to highlight general insights that are relevant to other ecological interactions."

**The distinction the whole review depends on is drawn in the second paragraph, before any evidence:**
> "Annual changes in host and parasite biology can generate outbreaks that occur around the same time each year, although it is important to distinguish observed seasonal outbreaks of infectious diseases from seasonal drivers of epidemic parameters."

Pattern → driver is the confusion the review exists to prevent, and it is separated on page one.

## Methods
A review has no methods section, but it has an organising device, and this one is explicit about it:

> "A useful framework for considering the mechanisms by which seasonality affects epidemiological systems is to examine variation in R₀, the basic reproductive number of a parasite."

**Choose one quantity and organise the whole review around what feeds into it.** R₀ is then taught from first principles for the mixed readership — "In a deterministic system, R₀ defines a break-even point (i.e. R₀ = 1) above which a pathogen can spread and below which a pathogen declines to extinction (Anderson & May 1991). Expressions for R₀ capture processes that give rise to new infections multiplied by the duration over which infectious stages are produced." — and every mechanism in the review is then located as a seasonal effect on one of its components.

**Model behaviour is demonstrated with figures whose parameters are given in the caption**, so the dynamical claims are reproducible: "The three curves represent infection prevalence through time from a term-time forced model (as used in Fig. 4) with infectious periods of 5 days (black line), 1 month (solid grey line) and 1 year (dashed grey line). For all cases, R₀ = 10, population size = 5 × 10⁶". The caption also states the lesson: the figure demonstrates "that the dynamical consequences of seasonality are sensitive to the infectious period".

## Results — evidence synthesis
**The abstract enumerates the mechanisms rather than summarising them**, which is what makes it useful:
> "Empirical evidence points to several biologically distinct mechanisms by which seasonality can impact host–pathogen interactions, including seasonal changes in host social behaviour and contact rates, variation in encounters with infective stages in the environment, annual pulses of host births and deaths and changes in host immune defences."

"Biologically distinct" is the load-bearing phrase: the claim is not that seasonality matters, but that it acts through separable routes that call for different models.

**The range of outcomes is stated as a range, not a result**: "Mathematical models and field observations show that the strength and mechanisms of seasonality can alter the spread and persistence of infectious diseases, and that population-level responses can range from simple annual cycles to more complex multiyear fluctuations."

**Examples are deployed across host taxa deliberately**, humans beside wildlife, so the generality claim is earned: influenza and malaria beside mycoplasmal conjunctivitis in house finches, tick-borne encephalitis, West Nile virus and avian malaria "linked to marked losses in Hawaiian forest birds and North American corvids".

**Where the field disagrees, the review says so and does not adjudicate**: "Although exact connections between weather and infectious disease patterns are the subject of much debate (Hay et al. 2005), detailed information on…". This is the review convention — report the disagreement, cite it, move on.

## Literature
Author–year citations in the ecology convention, very dense, with multi-source parentheses carrying a whole body of work: "(e.g. Fretwell 1972; Wingfield & Kenagy 1991; Blank 1992)". Reviews are cited as entry points rather than re-summarised — "(Table 1, and reviewed in Dowell 2001)". Table 1 itself functions as the literature review for the empirical patterns, which keeps the prose free for mechanism.

## Voice
First-person plural for the authors' choices ("we review examples", "we identify several research priorities", "we hope to highlight"), impersonal for the evidence. Hedging is pervasive and specific — "arguably represent", "appear to have been clustered", "could be caused by a multitude of factors", "it is likely that". Claims about what is *not* known are as prominent as claims about what is:

> "Thus, while it has been well established that influenza outbreaks are highly seasonal, the particular causes remain uncertain and could range from human crowding to low humidity, cold weather or cycles in host immunity."

**Note the construction**: what is established, then what is not, then the candidate explanations enumerated rather than left vague. This is how to write "we don't know" without writing "little is known".

## Discussion and limitations
The limitation of the whole field is stated at the head of the final section rather than buried:
> "Despite the pervasive nature of seasonality and the interesting dynamics that result from seasonally forced systems, we are only beginning to understand how seasonal external drivers influence the majority of host–parasite systems."

Then the obstacles, each concrete:
- **Scale**: "One major difficulty in uncovering seasonal patterns and drivers of infectious diseases is identifying the relevant spatial scale for analysis… Some patterns of seasonality only become obvious at large spatial scales, particularly in systems where significant numbers of random factors can impact disease incidence at local scales."
- **Confounded timescales**: "epidemiologically relevant factors that operate on different time scales, such as changes in the pool of susceptible hosts or El Niño periods, can produce strong seasonal outbreaks in some years, and little or no disease in other years."
- **Why the gap matters for modellers specifically**: "As models that incorporate seasonality are sensitive to which parameters are externally forced, there is a pressing need to identify these mechanisms." The methodological consequence of the biological ignorance is spelled out.
- **Why the problem is hard, not just unsolved**: "A key problem here is that seasonality is so ubiquitous in nature that identifying the relevant environmental drivers and the parameters they influence becomes extremely difficult."
- **The methodological prescription that follows**: "combining experimental and modelling approaches will become increasingly important in teasing apart the roles of multiple environmental drivers and the processes they influence."

The closing paragraph does three things in sequence — concede the difficulty, state what remains to be understood, and escalate beyond the subject to why it is urgent:
> "In summary, while seasonality is a ubiquitous force in nature, the complexity of its role has required the development of much theoretical work to gather even a basic understanding of how seasonality affects population dynamics… Basic processes important to host–parasite interactions are also seasonal in many other ecological systems; thus, it is likely that seasonality plays a much broader role in general population dynamics than we currently know – and the prospect that global climate change will rapidly modify current patterns of seasonality provides a major and immediate challenge for future ecological research."

**The review ends on the challenge, not on a summary** — and the escalation (this system → ecology at large → climate change) is earned because the generality claim was set up in the first paragraph.

## The abstract, verbatim
> "Seasonal variations in temperature, rainfall and resource availability are ubiquitous and can exert strong pressures on population dynamics. Infectious diseases provide some of the best-studied examples of the role of seasonality in shaping population fluctuations. In this paper, we review examples from human and wildlife disease systems to illustrate the challenges inherent in understanding the mechanisms and impacts of seasonal environmental drivers. Empirical evidence points to several biologically distinct mechanisms by which seasonality can impact host–pathogen interactions, including seasonal changes in host social behaviour and contact rates, variation in encounters with infective stages in the environment, annual pulses of host births and deaths and changes in host immune defences. Mathematical models and field observations show that the strength and mechanisms of seasonality can alter the spread and persistence of infectious diseases, and that population-level responses can range from simple annual cycles to more complex multiyear fluctuations. From an applied perspective, understanding the timing and causes of seasonality offers important insights into how parasite–host systems operate, how and when parasite control measures should be applied, and how disease risks will respond to anthropogenic climate change and altered patterns of seasonality. Finally, by focusing on well-studied examples of infectious diseases, we hope to highlight general insights that are relevant to other ecological interactions."

The six-move shape for a review: ubiquity → why this system → what the paper does → the mechanisms, enumerated → what the models show, with the range of outcomes → why it matters applied → the generalisation claim. **No result is withheld and no mechanism is left as "various factors".**

## Distinctive moves to borrow
1. **Organise a review around one quantity** — here R₀ — and locate every mechanism as an effect on one of its components. The framework is stated explicitly as a framework.
2. **Separate the pattern from the driver on page one**, when the field routinely conflates them.
3. **Enumerate mechanisms in the abstract** rather than summarising them. "Biologically distinct" tells the reader these need different models.
4. **Carry the vocabulary in a glossary** when the audience spans fields that do not share terms.
5. **Make the research agenda a section with imperative sub-headings**, so it can be lifted and used.
6. **Let a comparative table demonstrate generality** across hosts and pathogens, keeping the prose for mechanism.
7. **Say what is established, then what is uncertain, then list the candidate explanations.** Never "little is known".
8. **Give figure parameters in the caption** even in a review, so the dynamical claims can be reproduced.
9. **Mark the strongest claim as arguable** when it is — "arguably represent the strongest and most ubiquitous source".
10. **Report disagreement in the literature without adjudicating it**, with the citation that documents the debate.
11. **Close on the challenge and escalate beyond the subject**, having set up the generalisation in the opening.
